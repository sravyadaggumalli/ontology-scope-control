\begin{abstract}
LLM-based conversational agents are usually told what they may respond to through a free-text system prompt, most often a list of forbidden topics. Such prompts cannot be checked for contradictions, miss cases their author did not anticipate, and do not let a permission on a broad category carry down to the narrower cases inside it. This project tests whether an ontology in which permissions are attached to concepts and inherited through the hierarchy makes better allow/refuse decisions for a city 311 assistant than a prose prompt, a blocklist, or a flat topic list, on the same set of questions. A pilot evaluation of the two baselines on 42 questions shows them failing in opposite directions: the flat list refuses nothing in scope but lets near-miss questions through, while the prompt catches near misses but refuses hazardous-but-routine requests as emergencies.
\end{abstract}

\begin{IEEEkeywords}
conversational agents, guardrails, ontology, OWL, scope control, LLM
\end{IEEEkeywords}

\section{Introduction}

Today it is common practice to tell LLM-based conversational agents what they may respond to through a free-text system prompt, and those instructions are usually a list of things the agent must not do. I ran into three problems with this while building agent guardrails on Azure AI Foundry for a personal project. There is no good way to check a prose prompt for contradictions or gaps except by trying questions one at a time. A blocklist can miss related cases its author did not anticipate. And permissions do not inherit: allowing a broad category does not reliably allow the narrower cases inside it unless each one is spelled out. Vendor guardrails cover fixed harm categories like hate or self-harm, not what a specific agent is supposed to be \emph{for}.

This project analyses whether a structured scope specification, an ontology where permissions are attached to concepts and a permission on a parent concept carries down to its children, makes better allow/refuse decisions than a prose prompt or a blocklist, and what it costs to build it. The point is not the use of an ontology by itself but the comparison: whether hierarchy and inheritance give better conversational scope control than prompt-based and flat approaches on the same questions.

\section{Background and Related Work}

Table~\ref{tab:related} summarizes the eight papers reviewed. They fall into three groups.

\subsection{Runtime policy enforcement for tool-using agents}
The most active recent work puts a policy layer between an LLM agent and the actions it takes. AgentSpec~\cite{agentspec} writes rules as trigger, check, and enforcement, evaluated before each tool call and kept outside the model; it blocks over 90\% of unsafe code executions in its benchmarks at millisecond overhead. FORGE~\cite{forge} treats policy as a cross-cutting concern in the aspect-oriented sense: a weaver instruments the agent system and a reference monitor checks each action against Datalog rules over a dependency graph, with static checks for contradiction and redundancy. Agent Behavioral Contracts~\cite{abc} bring design-by-contract to agents with preconditions, invariants, and recovery. Ray~\cite{ray2026} asks what a gate in front of tool calls can guarantee at all and formalizes the trade-off between false blocks and misses. These systems share two things: they govern which tool an agent may call, not what it may say, and their rules are flat or Datalog, so a permission at a general concept does not carry down to specific cases. What I take from them is the placement of the policy outside the agent, applied at every decision point.

\subsection{Staying on topic in dialogue}
A second group treats scope as a dialogue problem. NeMo Guardrails~\cite{nemo} lets developers write topical rails: a user message is mapped to a canonical form by embedding similarity and a dialogue flow decides what happens next. The topics are a flat list matched by similarity, which is what my flat-list baseline is. CantTalkAboutThis~\cite{ctat} fine-tunes the model on synthetic dialogues with distractor turns across nine domains; its human-written distractors turn out much harder than the synthetic ones (GPT-4-turbo reaches F1 0.68 on them). EnSToM~\cite{enstom} keeps small models on topic without training by steering activations at inference, raising distractor accuracy on LLaMA-2-7B from 0.28 to 0.81. These papers measure the same two errors I measure, off-topic questions answered and on-topic questions refused. The difference is where the scope lives: inside the model weights or activations in the last two, with nothing to inspect or check, or as a flat list with no inheritance in NeMo.

\subsection{Ontology-based agent policy before LLMs}
The idea of expressing agent policy in OWL goes back to the early days of semantic web technologies. KAoS~\cite{kaos} represented authorizations and obligations as OWL classes, used a reasoner to find which policies applied to an action, and detected and resolved conflicts between policies, with lookups under a millisecond in deployments of over a thousand agents. Rei~\cite{rei} took a similar approach for pervasive computing. This work targeted the software agents of its time and was never compared against prompt-based control, since LLM agents did not exist. It is the approach mine builds on. The KAoS authors also noted that OWL-DL alone could not express some policies they needed, which is one reason I keep the permission lookup in a small procedural step and use the reasoner for hierarchy consistency and conflict detection.

\subsection{Gap and contribution}
The first group enforces actions, not conversation. The second handles conversation but either inside the model or as a flat list. The third has hierarchy and conflict detection but predates LLMs and was never measured against prompting. In the papers I reviewed I did not find an empirical comparison of a hierarchical, checkable scope specification against prompt-based control for conversational scope. That is what this project does: the same questions, the same embedding model, a prose prompt, a flat list, and an ontology with inherited permissions, so that the effect of the hierarchy can be measured on its own.

\section{Research Questions}

\textbf{Q1.} Does an ontology with inherited permissions let fewer out-of-scope questions through than a prose prompt, a blocklist, or a flat topic list?

\textbf{Q2.} Does it do that without wrongly refusing more in-scope questions?

\textbf{Q3.} When it fails, is the failure in matching the question to a concept or in the policy itself?

\section{Project Challenges}

The hardest part is matching a user's question to the right concept in the ontology. If the match is wrong, the reasoning on top of it cannot help, so I will measure this by running the ontology once with automatic matching and once with the correct concept supplied from my labels. When nothing matches, the system refuses, which is right for out-of-scope questions but wrong for in-scope ones, and I will report how often that happens. The prose and blocklist baselines need to be strong for the comparison to mean anything, so I will tune them on a small held-out set before the test set is fixed. Finally, the Dallas export I have covers only one department, so I pull the full request-type list through an aggregate query on the open data portal.

\section{Dataset}

I use the Dallas 311 Service Requests open data, October 2020 to present~\cite{dallas311}, in aggregate only: one grouped query returns department and request-type counts (435 rows, about 2.6 million requests). After stripping department codes from the type names, merging duplicate department labels, and dropping test records, 270 services remain across 23 departments, 201 with at least 50 requests (Fig.~\ref{fig:taxonomy}). No individual records or locations are used.

The pilot evaluation set has 42 hand-written questions in four groups: direct (11), inheritance-dependent (10), paraphrase (10), and out-of-scope (11), each labeled allow or refuse with its concept. The out-of-scope group is built from near misses that reuse 311 vocabulary. The full 160-question set with a second labeler is scheduled for Week 6.

\section{Proposed AI Approach}

Each approach takes a question and returns \textbf{allow} or \textbf{refuse} with a short reason. No chat interface or answer text is built; the decision is what is evaluated. Approaches A and R serve as the baselines.

\begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{1pt}
  \item \textbf{(A) Prose prompt.} A system prompt describing what the assistant covers, run through a small hosted LLM.
  \item \textbf{(B) Prose + blocklist.} Same prompt plus an explicit list of forbidden topics.
  \item \textbf{(R) Flat list.} The same concepts as the ontology, no hierarchy, matched by embedding similarity. No LLM.
  \item \textbf{(C) Ontology (proposed).} 60--100 concepts, three levels deep, built in Prot\'eg\'e from the Dallas request types, each marked permitted or denied.
  \begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{0pt}
    \item Match the question to the closest concept with a small sentence-embedding model (e.g.\ \texttt{bge-small-en-v1.5}); no match above threshold means refuse.
    \item Walk the concept's ancestors in Python with a most-specific-wins rule, so a parent's permission covers its children unless a child overrides it.
    \item An OWL reasoner via \texttt{owlready2}~\cite{owlready} keeps the hierarchy consistent and flags any concept marked both permitted and denied.
  \end{itemize}
  \item \textbf{(D) Ontology with gold concept.} Same as C but the correct concept comes from my labels, skipping matching. A diagnostic upper bound, not a usable system.
\end{itemize}

\section{Evaluation Plan}

\begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{1pt}
  \item \textbf{Metrics}, per approach and per question group on the 120 test questions: out-of-scope questions allowed; in-scope questions refused; for the ontology, how often a question matched any concept and how often an acceptable one.
  \item \textbf{Comparison:} McNemar's test between approaches (all see the same questions), plus a table of which questions each approach got wrong.
  \item \textbf{Qualitative:} the inheritance path used for questions that depend on it; the reasoner catching a concept marked both permitted and denied before any question is run.
  \item \textbf{Expected result:} the ontology does best on inheritance-dependent questions, loses some on reworded questions to matching errors, and differs little on questions that name a service directly.
  \item \textbf{Schedule:} taxonomy, questions, and ontology by Week 6; runs and analysis Weeks 7--9; report after that. API should cost under \$30; everything else runs on my computer.
\end{itemize}

\section{Baseline Results}

The primary baseline is the prose system prompt, which is what AgentSpec~\cite{agentspec}, FORGE~\cite{forge}, and NeMo Guardrails~\cite{nemo} all compare against and what practitioners deploy. The second is a flat list of concepts matched by embedding similarity, the same approach NeMo's topical rails use; it is built from the same concepts, embedding model, and threshold as the ontology arm, so the only difference between the flat list and the ontology arm is the hierarchy.

Both were evaluated on the 42-question pilot set (Table~\ref{tab:baseline}, Fig.~\ref{fig:baseline}). The prompt model (gpt-6-luna) does not accept a temperature setting, so it was run three times and the majority verdict scored; 3 of 42 verdicts changed between runs. The flat list was run with two concept lists: the pilot list, whose descriptions I wrote by hand after the questions existed, and a second list whose descriptions are taken verbatim from the Dallas service names with no wording of my own. The second list is the fair comparison, because its descriptions were written without any knowledge of the test questions. A higher threshold (0.65) was also tried to see whether it separates near misses from in-scope questions.

\section{Discussion of Baseline Results}

\textbf{Main findings.} The two baselines fail in opposite directions. The flat list refused none of the 31 in-scope questions but allowed 5 of the 11 out-of-scope ones, and every miss was a near miss that reuses 311 vocabulary: the neighbor's tree matched tree maintenance, the pothole in Plano matched pothole repair, the dog-bite lawsuit matched animal neglect. Similarity matching gets the topic right and ignores location, intent, and who is responsible.  Where as the prompt version allowed only the Plano pothole but refused 6 in-scope questions, and in every case its stated reason was that a hazard (a bent guardrail, a limb hitting trucks, a chained dog) was an emergency. Dallas 311 has request types for all of these. The same pothole with and without the words "cars keep hitting it" got opposite verdicts, so the wording triggers the refusal, not the topic. Replacing my hand-written concept descriptions with the city's own service names barely changed the flat list's verdicts, but 12 of 31 in-scope questions then matched the wrong concept; the verdicts held only because most concepts are permitted, so a wrong match still returned allow. Moreover raising the threshold to 0.65 did not separate near misses (scores 0.66 to 0.73) from real requests (down to 0.60). The prompt is also not repeatable due to non-determinism: 3 of 42 verdicts changed across three runs, and its reasons varied even when the verdict did not.

\textbf{Limitations.} This is a pilot on 42 questions, all written and labeled by me. The hand-written concept list was built after seeing the questions, which is biased for the flat list; the taxonomy-only list corrects this and is the one to be used from now on. The threshold was picked as starting value rather than tuned on a held-out split. One embedding model and one LLM were used, and three prompt runs is a small sample of its variability. The numbers just show us the direction for now not any meaningful differences statistically.

\textbf{What should be improved.} I have piloted with 42 questions but the real experiment needs to be conducted with a larger dataset. The questions I aimed initially is 160 with a tuning/test split, questions written by someone who is unaware of the ontology, plus a second labeler on a sample. The threshold should be tuned on the tuning split only and then frozen. For the ontology, concept accuracy matters more than verdict accuracy, since inheritance starts from the matched concept; with taxonomy-only descriptions it is 61\%, so the ontology's labels need alternate names drawn from the Dallas data rather than from the test questions. The pilot also showed two design requirements: hazardous-but-routine concepts must sit under permitted parents, separate from a denied Emergency concept so that they inherit permission, and denied concepts such as OtherJurisdiction and LegalMatter must be matched explicitly so near misses are refused with a valid reason. Finally, the ontology should be reported alongside the oracle condition with the gold concept supplied, to separate matching error from policy error.

\section{Expected Contribution}

A small, reproducible comparison of prose, blocklist, flat-list, and hierarchical scope policies on a constructed 311 benchmark, released with the question set and ontology. It will show whether inheritance helps, how much is lost to automatic matching, and that a structured policy can be checked for conflicts before use. If a well-tuned prompt matches the ontology at this scale, that is still a useful result about when a formal specification is worth the effort.
